Everything in a tea room is measured from where the host kneels. Move him and every distance moves with him. So this is that plan, drawn true to the millimetre, with the utensils at their own size and free to be picked up and set down. It is an instrument, not a diagram: it will not tell you where a thing goes, it will tell you exactly where you have put it.

if you practise

          
Take the placing shown as ours and not a
          school's. It is where we set the utensils down for one moment of
          one procedure, in one room, and a temae is a thing that moves: a plan
          can only ever hold a single instant of it. What this page offers a
          practitioner is not the arrangement. It is the surface: lay out what
          you were taught, read the distances off it in centimetres from the
          knees, keep it in a bookmark, hand it to someone as a link, print it
          as a sheet.


          
how it is drawn

          
A Kyoto mat is 1910 by 955 millimetres. Four of them
          turning about a half mat come to a square of exactly three mat
          widths, 2865 each way, which is why four and a half is the
          number and why the half mat is at the centre rather than tucked in a
          corner. The hearth is a 420 millimetre square, a little under
          one and two fifths shaku.

          
Every utensil is at its measured footprint and not at
          the size of a symbol: the water jar 175 across, the kettle 250, the
          lid rest 46, the tea scoop 185 long. If two of them overlap on this
          plan they would touch in the room. The drawing and the room in three
          dimensions read one table, so the two cannot drift apart,
          and a correction is a number in one place.


          
the mats, and which names are the school's

          
Three of the names on this plan are Urasenke's, out of
          the glossary of their own guidebook: the temaedatami, which
          the host works from and which is tinted here; the
          kyakudatami, the guests'; the fumikomidatami,
          stepped in from the crawl-in door, the nijiriguchi, which is the gap
          drawn in the lower wall. The two remaining labels, tokonoma-mae and
          the half mat, are descriptions of ours rather than terms of the
          school, and are marked as such because a beginner cannot tell our
          words from theirs by looking.


          
what the hearth decides

          
A room is classified by where the hearth is
          cut, and this one is the position Urasenke calls
          yojohangiri. The hearth then decides the host: their
          glossary gives the rule at uchizumi, that the corners of the
          hearth frame nearest the utensil mat serve as aiming points for the
          host's prescribed sitting position. So his facing is not a matter of
          taste, and the small vermillion dot on the frame is that corner.

          
One distance on the plan comes from the book and is
          drawn faint, in grey, so it can be used or ignored: the water jar is
          set down 24 centimetres in front of the knees. It follows
          the host when he is moved, because it is a relation and not a
          place.

what this plan cannot say

          
Where the hearth belongs in a four-and-a-half is a
          question we have open with a teacher, and it is honest to
          say so on the page rather than in a comment. Our reading of the
          school's diagrams puts it at the corner of the half mat against the
          host's own, which is where it is drawn; but the book stages its
          procedures in an eight-mat room whose mats do not carry the same
          names, and a position does not transpose between the two by analogy.
          The host, likewise, was placed here by hand and not derived.

          
And only this one room can be placed on today. The
          other seven arrangements are drawn flat in the
          chashitsu chapter and standing in
          the room.
